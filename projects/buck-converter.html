100 W Synchronous Buck Converter with MPPT

A solar battery charger built from scratch. It steps a 16–24 V PV input down to 12 V at up to 100 W. I sized the power stage, designed the inductor, laid out a custom 4-layer PCB in Altium, wrote the MPPT firmware, and brought the board up one subsystem at a time.

01Specs & topology

The converter charges a 12 V battery from a PV panel, so it has to handle a wide input range and track the panel’s maximum power point. I used a synchronous buck, which replaces the freewheeling diode with a low-side MOSFET to cut conduction loss.

02Power stage

Switching frequency

The pre-made board ran at 100 kHz. For my custom board I kept the same large passives and added turns to the RM10 inductor, which gave me room to drop to 50 kHz. I swept 40, 50, and 60 kHz and chose 50 kHz: it had the best efficiency, and anything below about 30 kHz caused audible ringing. Lower overlap and Coss loss improved efficiency by about 0.8%.

Switches

Both switches see a worst-case stress of Vin,max = 24 V, so I chose the IRFZ48N (55 V, 64 A, TO-220), which gives about 2.3× voltage margin. I modeled losses asymmetrically: the high-side switch takes Coss and overlap loss, and both switches take conduction loss. An iterative thermal model scaled Rds,on with junction temperature, and each switch got its own heat sink.

Capacitors

Minimum capacitance from the ripple spec came out to 20.6 µF at the input and 3.5 µF at the output. At the input, a 470 µF electrolytic in parallel with five 10 µF MLCCs provides bulk capacitance, and the MLCCs take most of the ripple current away from the electrolytic’s higher ESR. I split the current by impedance to calculate each capacitor’s loss. At the output, two MLCCs were enough at 100 kHz, but after moving to 50 kHz the ripple crept just past 5%, so I added two more.

03Magnetics

The inductor current is mostly DC with a small ripple, so saturation, not core loss, set the design. I picked 3C95 ferrite for its high saturation flux density, sized the core with the core-geometry (Kg) method, and used solid 18 AWG wire because AC winding loss was negligible and Litz wire wouldn’t help. For the final design I filled the unused bobbin space with extra turns.

04Gate drive & sensing

Gate drive: an LM5109B half-bridge driver with a bootstrap supply for the high side, so no isolation or level-shifting supply was needed. I sized the bootstrap capacitor for less than 10% droop and its resistor to keep diode current spikes within rating. Each gate uses a split turn-on/turn-off network (parallel resistor plus diode): slow turn-on limits overshoot, and fast turn-off cuts overlap loss.

Sensing: the output voltage goes through a high-impedance divider into an LM358 buffer and then an RC filter before the ADC. Output current is measured across a 2.5 mΩ shunt with an LT1999 current-sense amplifier referenced to an ADR1581. An LDO powers both amplifiers, and decoupling sits at every IC.

05MPPT control

A TI C2000 microcontroller generates complementary PWM through its ePWM module, with configurable dead time. Duty cycle is the control handle that moves the operating point along the panel’s I–V curve.

The algorithm is perturb-and-observe with an adaptive step. It starts with a coarse duty step and counts direction reversals. Frequent reversals mean it’s near the MPP, so it switches to a fine step to reduce oscillation. After 50 reversals it returns to the coarse step, in case the MPP has moved (for example, an irradiance change) or it’s stuck at a local maximum.

I tried several more complex MPPT schemes based on papers, but one of the simplest versions tracked best on real hardware.

06PCB layout

Layout priorities, in order:

  • High di/dt loops. I placed the commutation capacitor right at the switches to shrink the switching loop, and routed the gate-drive return directly under its forward path to minimize parasitic inductance and ringing.
  • High dv/dt node. I kept the switch node small and short to limit parasitic capacitance and charge injection.
  • Power paths. Via-free DC return between the input and output jacks, an AC return that follows the top-layer current path, and poured ground planes.
  • Analog isolation. Sensing sits far from the gate drive, with a ground-plane cut that steers power return current away from it and short high-impedance nodes at the op-amp.

07PLECS loss model

Before building the board, I made a PLECS model of the full power stage so I could predict where the losses would go and check component choices at each operating corner.

  • Parasitics in the power stage. The model includes inductor ESR and output-capacitor ESR. The input bank is split into the electrolytic and MLCC branches, each with its own ESR, so the simulation shows how ripple current divides between them.
  • Gate signals with dead time. Complementary q1/q2 signals from a single PWM, with turn-on delays that create the dead time. Scopes on Vsw and switch currents confirm the switching behaves as expected.
  • Operating point. Averaged Vin, Iin, Vout, and Iout give Pin, Pout, total loss, and efficiency directly from the simulation.
  • Per-component loss breakdown. RMS current through each element, squared and multiplied by its resistance (Irms²·R), gives conduction loss for both switches, each input capacitor branch, the output capacitor, and the inductor. Subtracting the switch conduction losses from total loss isolates switching loss.

That breakdown is what pointed to conduction loss as the dominant mechanism at 50 kHz, which shaped the “what I’d change” list below.

08Bring-up & testing

I brought the board up in five stages: PWM → gate drive → power ramp → sensing → control. I validated each stage on its own before moving on, which made faults easy to isolate. The test setup used a DC supply in parallel with a PV panel as the source, an electronic load as the 12 V battery, and an auxiliary supply for the gate drive and sensing. For measurements I used an oscilloscope with voltage and current probes, a thermal camera, a multimeter, and an LCR meter.

The converter met every spec at all operating corners. My loss model matched my own sweep to within about 1%. The official bench measured lower, most likely from losses I didn’t model: body-diode conduction during dead time and copper resistance in the board.

10What I’d change

  • Target conduction loss first. At 50 kHz it dominated the loss breakdown. I’d choose lower-Rds,on switches and heavier (14 AWG) inductor wire.
  • Fix thermal management: mount the heat sinks properly and consider forced air.
  • Tighten the layout to shorten power-stage current paths.
  • Tune dead time and gate resistors to cut overlap and body-diode loss. I ran out of time for this.

The biggest takeaway was the value of systematic, stage-by-stage bring-up. It turned debugging from guesswork into isolating one variable at a time.
